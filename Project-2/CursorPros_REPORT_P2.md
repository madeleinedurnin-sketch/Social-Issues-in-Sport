__Introduction__

As technology becomes to take centerstage in most aspects of human life, it has begun to creep into the professional sport sector as sports are often at the forefront of human innovation and evolve alongside societal trends instead of behind them. With these shift in trends and fan appetite, professional sport is increasingly shaped by commercialization, digital media logic, and entertainment-centered product design. As leagues compete for fragmented attention, many organizations now package sport less as a traditional contest and more as a media event optimized for visibility, sponsorship, and short-form engagement to counteract the negative results of a perceived slower pace or poor fan engagement. Golf is not exception to this norm as the industry has experienced a potential decline in viewership and engagement investors and sport managers have been forced to invent ways to counteract these negative trends. Tomorrow’s Golf League (TGL) provides a clear case of this shift. Rather than reproducing the conventions of outdoor tournament golf, TGL emphasizes a technology-mediated, indoor, high-production format intended to reach contemporary audiences through spectacle, accessibility, and broadcast appeal. As a result, TGL is not only a competitive product as a professional sports league but also a highly commercialized media product promoted in online spaces where creators and audiences actively shape how the league is understood.

While the technology driven model and online visibility of TGL creates a variety of growth and development opportunities, there has also been evidence of questioning from the potential audience surrounding the legitimacy of the league and whether the commercialization of golf is detracting from the sport’s core characteristics. In golf, legitimacy is often tied to deeply rooted ideas of "real golf," including environmental context, format continuity, and historical identity. Therefore, the TGL model provides a unique opportunity to examine whether technology-driven modernization represents the future of sport, or if this brings in the legitimacy question of if this is a departure from what fans consider authentic golf. This project addresses that issue through the following research question: How do YouTube content creators frame TGL's commercialization of professional golf, and in what ways does audience discourse in the comments reflect public acceptance, resistance, or ambivalence toward a technology-driven, entertainment-first sport model?

__Related Literature__

Although the technology utilized by the TGL is new within the professional golf industry, the concepts of commercialization, authenticity, legitimacy, and technology driven fan opinions are not new within sport discourse. The following selections of related literature help gain a deeper understanding of the overall topic and connect the issue within the TGL sphere to larger sport discourse.

__Commercialization, Fandom, and Contested Meaning__

In a structured review, Winell et al. (2023) states that "the impacts of commercialization on fans relate to four different themes. These are (1) fan identity, (2) fan attitudes, (3) fan emotions and (4) fan behaviours." This framing is important for the present TGL study because it treats commercialization as a multidimensional social process rather than as a single positive or negative variable. Furthermore, other related work also emphasizes that fan response is context-dependent. Winell et al. (2023) explicitly sought "to develop a holistic understanding of extant studies addressing the impact of commercialization on fans of elite sports." This objective supports a key claim in this project that reactions to TGL should be expected to vary across acceptance, resistance, and ambivalence rather than fall into a simple pro/anti split. TGL is not simply a business case in the sense of how it should be examined and understood. It is a legitimacy case in which commercialization intersects with identity and authenticity narratives in public fan discourse. While the commercialized success of the TGL may have some influence on fan opinion, the perceived meaning that fans derive from watching the sport product shapes the overall attitudes and long-term success surrounding the league.

__Authenticity and Legitimacy in Fan Engagement__

Related scholarship links commercialization to authenticity and legitimacy judgments more directly. Winell et al. (2025) notes that elite football is often criticized as "too commercial, potentially damaging the perceived authenticity of the sport and reducing the willingness of fans to engage with their teams." However, they also find that "perceived commercialization of the football league is not necessarily bad for the perceptions of authenticity and fan engagement." This dual finding supports a central claim within the project as commercialization can produce both legitimacy threat and legitimacy opportunity, depending on how fans interpret the new format.

When applied to the case of TGL, this literature helps explain why comments such as "not real golf" or "future of golf" are analytically important signals of legitimacy work, not just casual reactions. It also supports the project's coding logic that separates acceptance, resistance, and ambivalence. Additional fan-culture scholarship reinforces why these legitimacy arguments are emotionally and symbolically loaded. Cottingham (2012) shows that fan communities are organized through emotion, symbols, and solidarity practices, which helps explain why discussions of "real golf" versus "fake golf" in TGL comments carry social-identity stakes beyond technical format preferences.

__Digital Platforms and Mediatized Sport Discourse__

Research on mediatized and platformed fandoms emphasizes that digital environments intensify symbolic contestation. Levental (2023) argues that "recent technical developments and the widespread use of social networks have led to the emergence of a variety of online communities built around common interests," and that, in sports communities, this process can shift fans from club-specific support to broader, socially mediated sport-identity positions. In the same abstract, Levental (2023) adds that "humor serves as a common thread that encourages interactions," while shared discourse practices help produce group-level identity and loyalty. As applied to this study, these insights support the claim that YouTube comments are not peripheral noise and that they are a key place where legitimacy meanings are publicly produced and contested. Sturm (2020) similarly highlights that digital sport fandom now includes highly participatory online engagement rather than purely in-stadium identity performance. This claim further supports the importance of examining audience sentiment through online engagement rather than focusing solely on in person sales and reactions. The rise in technology surrounding the sport product itself is mirrored by the increased importance of online opinions and the type of engagement that fans are having virtually.

__Gaps Addressed by this Study__

Much of the current commercialization-authenticity scholarship has concentrated on football contexts and broader fan populations. The present project extends that work in three ways. First, it examines golf, where legitimacy claims are often historically and culturally rigid. Second, it focuses on a newly introduced format whose identity is still being negotiated. Third, it uses a platform-specific design (creator framing plus audience comments on YouTube), allowing direct analysis of framing-reception dynamics in digital public discourse. By integrating commercialization, authenticity, and platform-discourse perspectives, this study contributes a focused account of how fan legitimacy is negotiated in the early cultural life of a technology-mediated sport product.

__Data and Materials__

This project utilized the two provided TGL datasets to examine both creator framing and audience discourse as it pertained to the central research question. The primary dataset for creator framing is TGL\_VIDEO.xlsx (n = 355), which includes textual fields such as video title, description, summary, and transcript-style text. The primary dataset for audience discourse is TGL\_COMMENT.xlsx (n = 6,102), which provides direct access to YouTube comment language and reaction patterns. Because the research question focuses on response to TGL's technology-driven entertainment model, a model-specific comment subset (n = 695) is used for the core acceptance/resistance/ambivalence breakdown. This subset isolates comments explicitly discussing the format rather than all incidental conversation.

To operationalize legitimacy discourse, this project uses multiple keyword dictionaries and various labels to help support classification of audience language as it relates to the research question. A sample of the keyword dictionary has been provided in the AI Verification folder within Project-2 on the GitHub Repository. The labeled dictionary organizes terms into the core code families LEGIT\_POS (e.g. acceptance/legitimizing cues), LEGIT\_NEG (e.g. resistance/delegitimizing cues), and LEGIT\_AMB (e.g. ambivalence, mixed, or uncertain cues). The creator-side unit of analysis is the video item and its associated textual metadata. The audience-side unit is the comment entry. This dual design allows parallel testing of whether creator narratives emphasize commercialization and technology, and whether audience language aligns with, resists, or problematizes those narratives.

As it relates to AI verification, the process was designed to improve consistency, transparency, and interpretive reliability at each stage of the workflow. First, field-level checks were used to confirm that core text columns were populated, normalized, and correctly mapped to the intended unit of analysis (video-level for creator framing and comment-level for audience discourse). Second, keyword families were reviewed iteratively to reduce overlap, remove ambiguous duplicates, and ensure that acceptance, resistance, and ambivalence categories remained conceptually distinct.

Additional validation steps included repeated spot checks of coded excerpts against their original context, cross-pass comparison of category counts after each refinement, and percentage-total checks to ensure that all category distributions were internally consistent. The process also used consistency checks across creator-side and audience-side outputs to confirm that patterns reported in the findings matched the underlying coding logic and summary counts. Together, these procedures provide a reproducible audit trail for how raw text was transformed into the final discourse classifications used in this report.

__Analytical Approach and Justification__

The present analysis uses three core constructs developed within the initial project plan. The first concept is legitimacy and whether TGL is framed as a valid form of golf competition. The second is technology which pertains to references to simulator, virtual play, screen-based performance, indoor stadium design, and innovation. The last concept is commercialization which includes references to business logic, media value, entertainment orientation, sponsorship, and growth. Each of these concepts relates to the present research questions and allows for a transparent bridge between theory and measurement. 

The first approach examines how YouTube creators present TGL in video-level text fields (i.e. title, description, summary, and transcript-style text). Using the construct dictionary, the analysis identifies whether a video includes commercialization framing, technology framing, or both simultaneously. Three descriptive indicators are produced: the prevalence of commercialization framing, the prevalence of technology framing, and the overlap between these two frames. The overlap is analytically important because it indicates whether creators present TGL as a combined innovation-business project rather than only a technical novelty or only a commercial product. This approach addresses the first half of the research question by establishing the dominant public framing environment that audiences encounter before entering comment discourse. If creator messaging repeatedly pairs technology with commercialization, then audience legitimacy responses can be interpreted against a clearly defined framing baseline.

The second approach analyzes audience interpretation by filtering comments for model-specific relevance and classifying them into acceptance, resistance, ambivalence, and neutral/other categories. The classification is rule-guided and tied to legitimacy language rather than broad emotional tone. In practice, comments are coded according to whether they affirm TGL as a valid direction for golf, reject it as inauthentic or commercially distorted, or express mixed/conditional judgment. The neutral/other category captures comments that are not clearly engaged in legitimacy evaluation, which helps prevent overclassification. This approach addresses the second half of the research question by showing whether audiences reproduce creator-side narratives, resist them through authenticity claims, or remain uncertain. Methodologically, this step treats legitimacy as a meaning-construction process, which is more precise for this case than treating all positive language as acceptance or all negative language as rejection.

The third approach adds contextual checks by comparing coded discourse patterns with available engagement indicators (e.g., likes) and distribution metadata (e.g., video type, where available). This is not designed as causal modeling, but it is designed to contextualize visibility. These checks help identify whether certain discourse forms appear more prominent in higher-attention spaces, and whether particular content formats are associated with different legitimacy patterns. In a platformed environment like YouTube, visibility and interaction shape which interpretations become socially amplified. As a result, this approach functions as a supporting interpretive layer that strengthens the overall argument about how framing and legitimacy negotiation unfold in practice.

A keyword-guided, rule-based strategy is appropriate for this project for three reasons. First, it is conceptually aligned with the research question because each construct (i.e. commercialization, technology, legitimacy stance) is explicitly operationalized before analysis. Second, it supports reproducibility by making coding criteria transparent and auditable across creator and audience datasets. Third, it fits the assignment scope by balancing analytical depth with methodological clarity. This design prioritizes construct validity and procedural clarity over model complexity. For a course-based social issues project, that tradeoff is appropriate: readers can clearly see how constructs were defined, how coding decisions were applied, and how conclusions follow from reported distributions. The three approaches are intentionally complementary, moving from creator framing, to audience interpretation, to visibility context. The present analytical approach intentionally avoids overclaiming. It does not infer population-level fan sentiment, does not treat engagement metrics as pure support signals, and does not claim to capture all rhetorical nuance in comments. Instead, it provides a structured map of legitimacy-related discourse under clearly stated assumptions and constraints.

__Results__

Across 355 analyzed videos, creator framing strongly emphasizes commercialization and technology. Commercialization-related language appears in 313 videos, or 88.2% of the sample. Technology framing appears in 278 videos, or 78.3%. The two frames co-occur in 243 videos (68.5%). These values indicate that creator narratives are not split between separate themes; they frequently integrate technology and market logic as a unified story about what TGL is and why it matters.

Among model-specific comments (n = 695), audience discourse is distributed across four categories. Resistance accounts for 173 comments (24.9%), acceptance for 156 comments (22.4%), and ambivalence for 63 comments (9.1%). The neutral/other category comprises 303 comments (43.6%). The most striking pattern is not simple positivity or negativity, but contestation. Resistance slightly exceeds acceptance, while a large neutral/other segment suggests many viewers discuss TGL without adopting a clear legitimacy stance.

When taken together, results show a framing-reception asymmetry. Creators are comparatively unified in portraying TGL as an innovation-commercialization package, while audience responses remain fragmented. This implies that strong promotional coherence does not automatically produce legitimacy consensus among viewers. In this dataset, legitimacy is actively negotiated rather than settled. 

__Interpretation and Discussion__

The evidence indicates that YouTube creators frame TGL in strongly commercial and technological terms, often presenting innovation and entertainment value as central features of the league's identity. Audience discourse, however, reflects a mixed legitimacy response. Some viewers adopt future-oriented acceptance language, others resist through authenticity claims, and a meaningful portion remains ambivalent or noncommittal. Accordingly, the research question is answered in two parts as creator framing is relatively coherent and modernization-oriented, while audience reception is contested and socially divided around what counts as legitimate golf. This pattern directly matches the literature framework in as described in the related literature section as it is demonstrated that commercialization is interpreted by fans through identity, attitudes, emotion, and behavior rather than through a single uniform response.

Resistance appears consistent with the authenticity-threat dynamic described in the literature. Language framing TGL as artificial, gimmicky, or outside "real golf" aligns with concerns in Winell et al. (2025) that commercialization can be perceived as damaging authenticity and lowering willingness to engage. In these comments, legitimacy standards are anchored in tradition and inherited sport norms. Acceptance discourse appears tied to innovation pragmatism and entertainment value. Supportive comments suggest that, for some fans, format evolution is not a betrayal but a necessary adaptation to changing consumption patterns. This aligns with the authenticity-opportunity side of Winell et al. (2025), where commercialization can be interpreted positively when fans perceive quality, relevance, or future viability.

Ambivalence is analytically important. Rather than treating it as weak sentiment, it can be interpreted as transitional legitimacy work, which is consistent with the multidimensional fan-response logic summarized by Winell et al. (2023). Ambivalent audiences may acknowledge novelty and potential while withholding full endorsement pending proof of competitive seriousness or long-term sustainability. For league strategy, this group may represent the most influenceable segment.

The creator-audience gap highlights a broader social issue of legitimacy in contemporary sport is increasingly negotiated in platformed spaces where media logics shape discourse visibility. YouTube creators can amplify a coherent modernization narrative, but audience comments re-open meaning through contest, humor, skepticism, and identity signaling. This interpretation is consistent with Levental (2023) and Sturm (2020), which position digital fan spaces as active sites of identity formation and participatory meaning-making. This dynamic has implications for how sport change is governed. Legitimacy is no longer produced only through institutions (e.g. leagues, governing bodies, broadcasters) but through distributed discourse involving creators and publics. In such environments, persuasive success depends not only on promotion volume but on resonance with fan value frameworks and group-symbolic identity commitments, echoing Cottingham's (2012) fan-culture argument.

For leagues and media partners, the results suggest that innovation communication should include explicit legitimacy bridges to tradition, not only novelty framing. Claims about speed, excitement, and technology may attract attention, but resistance may persist if audiences interpret these features as displacing the core meaning derived from the sport. For athletes and brands, participation in new formats involves symbolic positioning as well as performance. Stakeholders may need to articulate how new products preserve competitive integrity while evolving delivery. For researchers, TGL illustrates how legitimacy disputes can serve as early indicators of whether innovation-first formats will stabilize culturally or remain niche spectacles. Overall, this case supports a key argument in social issues in sport that commercialization and technological change do not simply modernize sport, they reconfigure who gets to define authenticity and on what terms.

__Ethical Reflections and Limitations__

The project plan for this study commits to treating YouTube video and comment data as publicly available yet still ethically sensitive. People who post may not expect their words to be used in academic analysis, which creates a risk of identification through usernames, profile-linked details, or lengthy direct quotation. In line with that sentiment, this report favors anonymization and paraphrase in any illustrative use of audience language and avoids unnecessary identifiable information.

A second concern is harm. Comment and creator discourse can include commentary tied to exclusivity, wealth, demographics, or other sensitive themes. Presenting such content without care could unintentionally reinforce stereotypes or amplify negative narratives as if they represented all fans. The analysis therefore treats contentious or stereotype-laden language as material to interpret critically, not as neutral evidence of what “the public” believes, and it does not imply that views expressed in these threads are representative of every TGL viewer or golf audience.

Bias and reflexivity are also considered within the ethical scope. Selection and visibility on the platform are shaped by algorithms and by which videos draw attention, so highly visible threads may overrepresent stronger or more extreme opinions than a random fan would hold. Personal and tool-assisted judgment also enter through keyword choices, coding rules, and AI-supported steps in the workflow. The previously submitted project plan emphasizes transparency about those limits rather than overstating certainty.

Interpretation of findings is bounded by limitations identified within the initial research plan as consideration of those factors shaped how data was examined. First, YouTube does not stand in for the full audience of TGL. Commenters are a self-selected subset, and comment sections often attract more opinionated participation, which can make discourse look more polarized than attitudes across all consumers of the sport. Claims in this report therefore describe patterns in this corpus and platform context, not universal public opinion. Second, comments are easy to misread when context is thin. Jokes, irony, and sarcasm are difficult for automated or rule-based coding to capture reliably, so some utterances may be sorted into the wrong category or missed entirely. That constraint is especially relevant given the use of dictionary-guided and AI-assisted processing utilized in this project as outlined by the project guidelines. Third, engagement signals such as likes reflect visibility and interaction, not a clean measure of support, agreement, or legitimacy. A video or thread with high engagement may concentrate both praise and criticism, so engagement cannot be read as endorsement of TGL or of any single framing. Where subgroup comparisons rely on video metadata, missing or uneven fields further narrow how far those patterns can be generalized. These limitations do not invalidate the descriptive patterns reported here, but they define the scope within which those patterns should be read, as structured evidence about creator framing and audience legitimacy discourse on YouTube for this dataset, not as a definitive census of fan belief.
